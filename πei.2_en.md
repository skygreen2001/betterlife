  - A note:
  - We need to understand "∞" and "...":

    **In ordinary mathematics (the real numbers and limits):**
    $
    1+2+3+\cdots
    $
    is a **divergent** infinite series. It does not converge and therefore has **no ordinary sum**. In other words, we cannot simply say that it equals a real number.

    If you write:
    $
    \lim_{n\to\infty}(1+2+3+\cdots+n)
    $
    then:
    $
    1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2
    $
    As $(n\to\infty)$, this tends to $(+\infty)$. So we can say:
    $
    1+2+3+\cdots+n \to +\infty
    $
    Strictly speaking, however, **"\(1+2+3+\cdots+\infty\)" is not a well-formed expression**.

    **In certain mathematical contexts that redefine summation:**
    Methods such as Ramanujan summation and analytic continuation can yield counterintuitive results, for example:
    $
    1+2+3+\cdots = -\frac{1}{12}
    $
    This is not an ordinary sum in the usual sense of addition. It is a "regularized" result obtained through specific mathematical tools and cannot be used directly in everyday mathematics.

    The conclusion is:

    - **Ordinary mathematics: divergent, with no finite sum; it tends to $(+\infty)$.**
    - **In non-ordinary or regularized interpretations: it is sometimes written as $-\frac{1}{12}$, but the meaning is different.**

    If this is just a primary- or secondary-school question, the answer is: **the expression is invalid; infinity cannot be added to in order to obtain an ordinary number.**
